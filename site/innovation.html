Approach · for a decision maker weighing what is actually new

Zane makes five claims about what it adds, and each claim comes with the instrument that shows it and the limit that bounds it.

A claim on this page is a structural statement about what Zane adds, given with an instrument and a limit, so that a reader can test the claim rather than accept it. Each claim lets a reader run a command, or open a file or a page, to check it. The claims are structural, and their only evidence is this repository. There is no study, no user, and no application of anyone else's run end to end. Each claim below says where a reader can see it and what limits it.

How each claim is stated, and what the claims do not say

A claim of novelty costs nothing to make, and a claim with an instrument can be tested. Each of the five claims has a command, a file or a page that a reader can run or open, and a limit written beside it.

The claims concern structure: how the pieces are arranged, and what a tool can therefore be made to do. They do not claim that interfaces built this way are better for the people who use them. Nothing here measures that, and the practices that do measure it, usability testing above all, are not replaced (Comparison).

The claim that the unit of declaration is what a person is doing

The claim. A surface is declared by its intent, in a closed vocabulary of 22 verbs and 10 kinds of declaration, with no widget in it. A widget is a consequence of an intent and never a primitive.

The instrument. The instrument is the grammar and the verifier, with a suite of 73 fixtures whose verdicts are known. The Language page walks through the grammar, and What it refuses runs the verifier.

Limit: the vocabulary was closed by reasoning about interfaces, and it has been exercised on this project's own fixtures and sites. No team outside the project has declared one of its own products in it.

The claim that the law is data and that every finding cites it

The claim. The rules live in one versioned file that the Reader, the verifier and any linter load. Every refusal carries the rule, the article and the right it rests on, so a review is an audit and not an argument.

The instrument. The instrument is the contract's bindings: of 37 diagnostics, 17 cite at least one rule, 8 cite an article and no rule, and 12 cite nothing. The rules page shows one entry as the tools load it.

Limit: twelve diagnostics cite no rule and eight cite only an article, so the grounding is incomplete, and this page says so. Of the 123 rules, 80 still need a person's judgment.

The claim that refusal comes before anything is rendered

The claim. The verifier refuses by name a flow with no exit, an act with no undo and a promise that a channel cannot carry, from the declaration alone, before there is a screen to inspect.

The instrument. The instrument is the verifier and its suite: 46 of the 73 fixtures are refusals, and 22 are lawful twins that differ from a refusal by one change, so that a tool which refuses everything fails. A reader runs the suite with node tools/zane/conformance.js.

Limit: the claim covers only what a declaration can decide. Measuring a painted layout belongs to the audit, so the suite holds 0 fixtures at the level of a rendered surface, by design. This claim does not decide whether a person finds the result usable.

The claim that one declaration crosses channels, with the gap drawn

The claim. The same declaration is projected to 10 channels, a verb that a channel cannot carry is refused, and the places where the law has nothing to say are drawn empty and counted.

The instrument. The first instrument is the grid, on which 35 of 55 live cells hold no rule. The second instrument is the cross-channel check on the Channels page, which reports one pair of channels proved and one pair not proved.

Limit: nobody has perceived any channel for this grid, and the placement of rules on it changes with the reader: two blind readers agreed on both axes for a fifth to a third of the rules. The renderers that draw, speak or print are not part of this repository.

The claim that the language is itself declared, with two evaluators held to each other

The claim. Zane is written a second time as a declaration in Haro, and a court is forged from that declaration. Where the forged court and the hand-written verifier differ, each difference is named, and a ratchet fails on any other difference.

The instrument. The instrument is the Declared in Haro page, which shows that the two courts differ on 18 of the 73 fixtures and assigns each difference to one of 8 seams.

Limit: the forged court is not one of this repository's gates, because its judge needs a separate checkout, and it says clean to 10 fixtures that the verifier refuses or warns on. It shows what the meta-language can carry and counts what it cannot carry, and it replaces nothing.

What the claims do not show: no study, no user and no foreign application

  • No study. Nothing here measures whether a person does better with an interface made this way.
  • No user. No person has perceived any channel for the grid, and only the project's own people have looked at the pages.
  • No foreign application run end to end. The one real application planned as a case belongs to another project and has not been run, and the screenshot audit has never been run on a real application. The status board lists what exists, what is designed and what is held.

The claims are structural, and their evidence is the repository: files a reader can open and commands a reader can run. A claim that does not hold when it is run is a finding, and this page is then corrected.
