Approach · for anyone who asks what the law covers

Zane is designed around a conceptual grid of what a person does in every channel.

The grid crosses the six things a person does with the 10 channels an interface can use, and each cell holds the rules written for that activity in that channel. The grid ensures systematic coverage: an empty cell is a visible gap in the law, not a hidden one. Of the 55 cells that can hold a rule, 20 do and 35 do not yet.

The grid of channels and activities, with the rules on each cell

Each row of the grid is a channel, and each column is an activity a person does. A channel is what the language and the law call a medium, and the Channels page describes what each channel is made of. A cell shows how many rules are written for that activity in that channel, and how many of the suite's 73 fixtures sit on it.

A cell marked closed belongs to a channel that cannot carry any verb the activity owns, so a person cannot do that activity in that channel, and no rule is meant to sit there. A cell marked none is open and has no rule written for it, and each such cell is a finding.

The six activities that form the grid's columns

The grid has six activities. Each activity is a question a person asks, in the order a person meets an interface, and no activity is named after a defect:

ActivityThe questionVerbs it owns
perceiveIs there something, and what state is it in?none
understandWhat is this, where am I, what does it say?DISCOVER UNDERSTAND LOCATE READ SCAN SCROLL ZOOM COMPARE
decideWhat can I choose, and can I narrow it?FOCUS FILTER SELECT HIGHLIGHT PREVIEW MARK
actCan I do the thing, and know that it was done?ACT CONFIRM
recoverCan I go back, stop, try again, leave?CANCEL UNDO PAUSE RESUME RETRY EXIT
trustCan I rely on who or what is acting, and on what it tells me?none

The 22 verbs of the intent grammar are shared out once each among the activities, so the grid's columns are the language's own verbs grouped by what a person does, and not a classification invented beside the language.

Perceive and trust own no verb on purpose: a person senses a state before using any verb, and trust is a property of every verb. This choice keeps the grid true of a lamp, which has no READ verb and whose state a person still perceives across a room.

Cells a channel cannot carry, and why

A cell is closed when its activity owns verbs and the channel marks every one of those verbs absent. 11 cells of the grid are closed. They are derived from media.json in the way the verifier derives what a channel cannot carry: understand on haptic; decide on haptic, print, luminous and signage; act on print, luminous and signage; and recover on print, luminous and signage.

Closed cells are not counted as gaps. The Channels page shows what each channel is made of, and why it cannot carry these verbs.

The empty cells of the grid, listed as a finding

  • The law is written for a screen. 94 of the 121 placed rules are written for the screen, and 89 of them are written for nothing else. 26 rules are stated without a channel, and 6 rules name another channel.
  • 5 of the ten channels have no rule written for them: haptic, luminous, signage, spatial and terminal. A rule reaches these channels only when it is stated without a channel. Such a rule can be projected into them, but nobody has projected one and checked it, so this reach is not counted as coverage. Counting it would make the gap read zero.
  • A rule on a cell does not mean that anything checks the cell. 80 of the 123 rules need a person's judgment, and a rule that only a person can apply says nothing about whether a tool checks it.
  • Rules for the five uncovered channels are held, not advanced, until a product that ships in one of those channels is named. The grid shows where the law is silent, and it does not propose a rule.

Limit: nobody has heard, felt or looked at a channel to check a cell. The grid describes perception, so this phase is reported as designed and measured, and not as verified by a person.

How far the placement of rules on the grid can be trusted

The placement of the rules on the grid is one reader's first placement: each rule's activities are read from its title and its reason, and its channels from its wording. Another reader would move some rules, and the counts would move with them.

The placement is measured, not changed. Two readers who had not seen the placement re-placed samples of rules, and the table below counts the placements on which they agree. The figures were measured on 3 November 2026 against the placement of that day. A ruling has since moved four rules, so the comparison scripts give other figures today, and the saved outputs are the record.

The readWhat it re-placedActivities and channel both agree
First, a sample taken by positionevery fourth placed rule, by number6 of 31
Second, exactly the rules that decide the empty cellsevery rule stated without a channel, and every rule named for another10 of 33

The readers agree on both axes for between a fifth and a third of the rules. The first sample could not move the empty cells, because none of its rules sat on them, so it is a weak result and not a strong one.

The second sample could move the empty cells, and its placement would widen the gap by about five judgments about named rules. The gap is 35 cells on this placement and a little wider on that reader's placement, so the gap measures the placement and not the law.

Where the placements disagree, the file keeps its first placement and does not say which one is right. Each disagreement is a finding for the author.

How to report a rule that is missing from a cell

The grid supports a practice of six steps, and this site shows each step:

  1. Declare the surface in a .zane file: what the person is doing, and what stays always reachable. The Language page.
  2. Place it on the grid: which activities it serves, in which channels.
  3. Let the law refuse what it refuses, by name, before anything is drawn. What it refuses.
  4. Project it to each channel; a verb a channel cannot carry is refused too. Channels.
  5. Audit what was painted. The UI audit.
  6. File every defect found against a cell, so that it confirms the grid, extends it, or falls outside it, and never becomes a new rule on its own.

A discovered issue is filed as the record below, taken from doc/design/COVERAGE.md, so that it lands on a cell of the grid:

id:        COV-<year>-<number>        # stable, never reused
cell:      <activity> x <medium>      # one of the 66 cells, or
status:    reported | placed | confirmed | gap | extends | outside | closed
rules:     [<rule numbers on that cell the issue touches>]    # empty when the cell has none

An issue lands on the grid in one of three ways. It confirms a cell when a rule already forbids it, or when the cell has no rule and stays in the gap with a rule held for the author. It extends the grid when no row or column fits, and the new row or column is proposed to the author. It falls outside the grid when nobody is using an interface, as with a concern of the maker.

Evidence follows the perception gate: a defect a person would perceive stays unverified until a person has perceived it.

Limit: the place where these records are kept, and the person who files them, are not decided; only the grid is fixed.
