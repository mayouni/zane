Language · for people who build or judge interfaces

The basics of Zane are a language that declares what a user, a person or an agent, is doing on an interface, and a verifier that refuses by name what the law forbids.

In its basics, the Zane language declares the intent of a surface in a closed vocabulary of 22 verbs and 10 kinds of declaration, with no widget in it, so that a verifier can check the surface against the law before anything is drawn. The language lets a designer find a forbidden interface at design time, because the verifier refuses it and cites the rule it breaks. This page describes the four steps from a declaration to a measured page, which of them exist today, and where each one stops.

How a declaration differs from a published guideline

The practices that teams already use are good at what they do. They differ from the law in the moment at which they apply and in whether anything can refuse a result.

A published guideline

A widely used set of ten usability heuristics asks, among other things, that a user can always leave a state they did not mean to enter and take back what they did. The page presents them as broad rules of thumb, first developed for heuristic evaluation, in which reviewers judge an interface against them.

The heuristics name no pass or fail, so a review finds what a person can see and judge, and nothing in the heuristics refuses a build.

The heuristics, at Nielsen Norman Group

The same idea, as a law

// delete_invoice.zane
DEFINE INTENT_FLOW delete_invoice (
  SATISFIES [SELECT, CONFIRM, ACT],
  ALWAYS_REACHABLE [CANCEL, EXIT]
)
$ zane check delete_invoice.zane
delete_invoice.zane:2  error  MISSING_UNDO  delete_invoice
    An act declares no way back: SATISFIES holds ACT, ALWAYS_REACHABLE lacks UNDO.
    Rule 79 · The Undo Covenant · Article IV · the right to reverse

1 error(s), 0 warning(s) across 1 file(s) at level 2

The heuristic and the law are both right. The heuristic judges what only a person can judge, and the law refuses what a machine can see before a pixel exists. This site does not claim that the law replaces the heuristic.

A declaration refused by the verifier, and the same declaration corrected

The declaration below describes a payment approval before a single pixel exists. It contains no widget, no layout and no framework.

// payments.zane — design phase, day one
DEFINE INTENT_FLOW approve_payment (
  SATISFIES [SELECT, CONFIRM, ACT]      // interest → review → commitment
)
$ zane check payments.zane
payments.zane:2  error  MISSING_UNDO  approve_payment
    An act declares no way back: SATISFIES holds ACT, ALWAYS_REACHABLE lacks UNDO.
    Rule 79 · The Undo Covenant · Article IV · the right to reverse
payments.zane:2  error  MISSING_CANCEL  approve_payment
    A flow that acts cannot be abandoned: ALWAYS_REACHABLE lacks CANCEL.
    Rule 26 · The Back Button is Holy · Article IV · the right to leave

2 error(s), 0 warning(s) across 1 file(s) at level 2

The verifier refuses this interface at design time, from its structure alone, and cites the law by number and title. When the declaration states the way back, the verifier accepts it:

DEFINE INTENT_FLOW approve_payment (
  SATISFIES [SELECT, CONFIRM, ACT],
  ALWAYS_REACHABLE [UNDO, CANCEL]
)
$ zane check payments.zane
clean — 1 file(s), 0 violations at level 2 against Zane v3.16

The output above is copied from a real run, and this site's own check re-runs it and fails if the page and the verifier disagree. The verifier is one file, zane, built from this repository, and it needs nothing else to run. The status board states what exists, what is designed and what is held.

The four steps from a declaration to a measured page, and which of them exist

Declare. A .zane file says which intents a surface satisfies and what stays always reachable. There are 10 kinds of declaration and 22 verbs. The vocabulary is closed on purpose, because a widget is a consequence of an intent and never a primitive. The language is also declared as data, and a court can be built from that data.

Refuse. The verifier reads the declaration against the law, which is data (123 rules, v3.16), and refuses what it forbids. Every refusal cites the rule, article or right it rests on. The refusal page reads one refusal line by line.

Project. One declaration can be rendered in more than one channel (what the grammar and the law call a medium), and a verb a channel cannot carry is refused. That check is built, and the media data is in conformance/media.json.

Measure. A declaration is not a page, so the UI audit measures what a browser actually paints: the size of text, the true contrast, the bars, the room a block has, and the menu against the tree.

The same law applied at design time, in development and at runtime

The parts of the language that exist today, and what each one reads

  • Exists: the grammar and the verifier (40 diagnostics), the law as data, the UI audit, and a suite of 77 fixtures that any implementation can prove it agrees with.
  • Data for every channel: the media carriage and the signal vocabulary, which the renderer of each channel reads.
  • Symbols you supply: the facts a declaration does not hold (the entities, actors, state and elements of a surface) come from a table you give the verifier, which reads it at level three.
  • Rules by channel: the rules for haptic, luminous, signage, spatial and terminal channels are written when a product that ships in one of them is named.

Every part, with its state, is on the status board; how to prove a tool agrees is on Conformance.
