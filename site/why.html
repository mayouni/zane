Law · for people deciding whether a law is worth having

The Zane law is a versioned file of rules that names the ways interfaces keep decaying, so that each one can be refused by name.

The Zane law is a set of rules for business interfaces stored as one versioned file, and it exists so that the recurring defects of interfaces are refused by citation rather than argued about. The law prevents a build from repeating an inherited convention that harms the person using the screen, because a tool or a reviewer can refuse the convention and cite the rule. This page sets out the case for the law in six steps, says where each step can be checked, and then says where the case is weak. The evidence is the author's own builds, recorded as they happened. There is no study, and no one outside the project has applied the law to a product of theirs. Of its 123 rules, a machine can refuse 11 today.

The inherited conventions that make interfaces decay

Most people have used software with the following defects. Its text is small and faint, so a person leans in to read it. Its buttons are labelled "OK" and do not say what they will do. A chat launcher floats over the paragraph a person is reading. A breadcrumb shows where a person came from but not where the person could go.

A table scrolls sideways next to twenty centimetres of empty page. A menu lists a page as top-level while the page's own path says that it lives two levels deep.

These defects are not new. They are inherited conventions: patterns that teams copy because they are everywhere, and keep because nothing in the process is required to refuse them. Each convention costs the person using the screen a little attention or a little confidence, for hours every day.

Where the newest rules of the law come from

The law in this project comes from building and not from a seminar room. Its newest rules were extracted, defect by defect, from real applications built with AI assistance in 2026. Each correction was recorded when it was made, and it was kept only if it generalised and a reviewer could decide it yes or no. Rules 105 to 124 each come from such a defect, and the table further down names the newest eight.

The working thesis is narrow. In those builds, a generator reproduced the inherited conventions quickly and confidently, even under an instruction to be careful, because nothing it was given had the standing to refuse them. The only correction that keeps pace with a generator is a rule that the generator can be made to check.

Limit: this record covers one author's builds. It shows that these defects occurred and were corrected, and it does not show how common they are in other teams' work.

What a law stored as data changes, and where to check each change

Guidelines in prose are good at what they do, and the Positioning page says where each one stops. A guideline alone cannot refuse a build. Storing the law as one versioned file changes this in five ways, and each change can be checked:

The law also has a home that prose lacks, which is a language. An interface is declared in Zane as what the person is doing and what stays reachable. The verifier refuses unlawful structure before anything is drawn, and the UI audit measures what was painted. The Language page describes the four steps.

What the law cannot do

  • Most of the law waits on a person. Only 11 of the 123 rules are refused by a conforming compiler today; 80 need a reviewer's judgment.
  • The law does not say whether a page is pleasant. The verifier checks a declaration and the audit checks what was painted. Usability testing decides whether a person finds the page usable, and nothing here replaces it.
  • The law is not shown to be right for other teams. Only this repository stands behind it: no study, no user and no foreign application run end to end.
  • The law has a stance. It puts business applications first, as the last section of this page explains.

The builds with AI from which the rules of the law were taken

Every correction below was recorded at the moment it was made, and it was canonized only if it generalised and a reviewer could decide it yes or no.

The banking product (April 2026) is a full agentic banking platform, built and corrected screen by screen. In its supervision tables the same green meant two different things two columns apart. Its card-styled tiles had to be poked before a person could learn what they did. Its studio stacked two rows of identical tabs, and a floating assistant masked the very work it offered to help with.

In a sibling project's i18n work (May 2026), languages were added by forking templates, which left French screens full of untranslated jargon until language became data. In the Refine rebuild (June 2026), a 22-section homepage that the author judged unreadable was rebuilt under a reading budget.

The StzWeb site (August 2026) produced twelve rules, from Rule 105 through 116, and each was canonized live from a defect that the generator produced while building the site that documents the law. The audit tool then caught its own depth-calculation defect on its first run in this repository. The law exempts nothing here, and that includes the tooling.

The stance of the law, which puts business applications first

The law is openly opinionated and puts business applications first. It serves operators rather than explorers: people who use the same screens eight hours a day under pressure, responsibility and fatigue. Delight on consumer sites is outside its jurisdiction, and the dignity of the operator is its whole concern. The law's own preamble states the stance in four sentences:

Calm is productive. Stability is humane. Clarity is respectful. Undo is dignity.
— The Zane Constitution, preamble
