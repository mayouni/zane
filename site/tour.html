Try · for presenting Zane to a team

The tour presents Zane in eight scenes, ordered for the people in the room.

The tour is a presentation of Zane in short scenes, for a presenter who shows Zane to a team. It lets a presenter show Zane in the order that the people in the room need: the presenter chooses a profile, and the scenes follow in that profile's order. The arrow keys or the space bar move between scenes, n shows the presenter's notes, f fills the screen, and Escape opens the page that a scene comes from. Every number in a scene is the same number as on the site.

The five profiles that the tour is ordered for

  • UX designer 8 scenes
  • Product designer 8 scenes
  • Programmer 8 scenes
  • AI builder 8 scenes
  • Decision maker 8 scenes

Each scene has its own address: tour.html#programmer/4 opens the fourth scene for a programmer, so a presenter can start at any scene and can send a link to it.

The stage, where the scenes of the tour are shown

The tour begins when a profile above is chosen.

Zane starts from what a person is doing, and an interface renders it.

A person does not come to an interface to click a button.

A person comes to find something, to compare, to act, to confirm and to undo.

Zane declares these activities and treats every screen as a consequence of them.

Presenter's notes. Start from the person and not from the screen. Ask the room what their users come to do, and write the verbs down.

The verifier refuses a flow that a person cannot leave, and names the rule.

DEFINE WIZARD account_opening (
  SATISFIES [UNDERSTAND, CONFIRM, ACT],
  ALWAYS_REACHABLE [UNDO, CANCEL],
  STEPS [identity, funding, review],
  ACTOR applicant
)
$ node tools/zane/cli.js check tour_account_opening.zane
tour_account_opening.zane:1  error  TRAPPED_FLOW  account_opening
    A multi-step flow has no reachable EXIT.
    Rule 51 · The Cognitive Tunnel · Rule 53 · Interaction Lockdown · Article II · the right to leave

1 error(s), 0 warning(s) across 1 file(s) at level 2
Presenter's notes. Point at the last line of the output, which gives the rule, the article and the right. Nothing has been drawn at this point.

The law is one file, and every refusal cites a rule from it.

The law holds 123 rules under 7 articles, and the rules guarantee 6 rights.

The tools load the same file that the reader reads, so the tools and the text cannot drift apart.

Only 11 rules are refused by a machine today, and 80 rules need a person's judgment.

Presenter's notes. The second number states the limit. Say it before someone asks.

Zane is designed around a grid of what a person does in every channel.

The grid crosses six activities with 10 channels.

Of the 55 cells that can hold a rule, 20 hold one and 35 hold none.

5 channels have no rule written for them at all.

Presenter's notes. The empty cells are the finding of the grid. A practice that cannot show where it is silent cannot be checked.

The law refuses a promise that a channel cannot carry, such as zoom on a phone line.

One declaration can serve many channels.

A flow that promises ZOOM over a voice line is refused, because a voice cannot carry it, and the same flow on a screen is clean.

4 of the channels cannot carry an act at all.

Presenter's notes. This scene is for anyone who ships voice, print or an API. The law refuses a promise, and it does not refuse the channel.

Zane works beside heuristics, standards and frameworks, and does not replace them.

Heuristics and usability tests judge what only a person can judge.

Accessibility standards and linters check a rendered page.

Frameworks build the screen.

In the sources this project read, none of these practices refuses a declaration, before a screen exists, for lacking what a person must be able to do.

Presenter's notes. Be fair to the practices in the room. The claim is narrow, and the comparison page lists its sources.

A run on a real site found four real findings and showed a limit of the audit.

The web case reads the navigation of the Softanza site and changes nothing.

The verifier refused the author's own first declaration, and the audit found four pages with no labelled path.

The audit also reported twenty-two contrast findings over a photograph.

Those findings are a limit of the audit and not defects of the site, and the web case page says which findings are which.

Presenter's notes. Every case on the site is a real run, and each case states its limits. This is the style of the whole project.

In CI, the verifier reports a refusal as an exit code.

The verifier exits 1 on a refusal, 0 when the declaration is clean and 2 on a usage error.

A build gate needs nothing more, and today the verifier needs only Node.

Presenter's notes. For programmers, show the exit code first, and then the envelope that a tool can read.

A grammar holds a generator to the shape of a declaration, and the court still judges its meaning.

A grammar file fixes the shape of a declaration (the 10 kinds and their clauses), so a model cannot write a declaration that does not parse.

The grammar cannot fix meaning: a flow with no exit is well formed and wrong, and only the court refuses it.

Presenter's notes. For AI builders, the grammar and the court are two different promises. Say which promise they need.

Agreement between verifiers is proved by running the fixtures.

The suite holds 73 fixtures with known verdicts, and 12 files are pinned by hash.

A verifier in any language is a peer, and the suite names each fixture on which a verifier disagrees.

Presenter's notes. Zane has no library to adopt. This fact surprises people, so state it and leave it without explanation.

The language is also declared as data, and two courts are held to each other.

A court forged from the declaration agrees with the hand-written verifier on 55 of 73 fixtures.

The other 18 fixtures are named, and a ratchet fails on any difference that is not named.

Presenter's notes. This scene is for the language people in the room. Skip it for everyone else.

Using Zane today costs Node, a browser and the time to write declarations.

Zane needs Node and a browser, and it needs nothing to install, no library to adopt and no framework to change.

A single file that needs neither Node nor a browser is designed and not built.

The work is writing declarations by hand for the surfaces that matter.

Presenter's notes. For decision makers, the cost is an afternoon per surface. The real risk is that most of the law still needs a reviewer.

The project has not yet shown a study, a user or a foreign application.

The project has run no study, has involved no user, and has run no foreign application end to end. The claims are structural, and their evidence is the repository: files a reader can open and commands a reader can run.

Nobody has perceived any channel to check the grid.

Presenter's notes. Always end on the limits before the next step. The limits make the rest believable.

The next step for a UX designer is to read the grid.

The grid shows where the law speaks and where it is silent, and a UX designer files the next defect they see against one of its cells.

Presenter's notes. Leave the room with the grid and with the record that a defect is filed as.

The next step for a product designer is to declare the surfaces of their own product.

A product designer picks three surfaces of a product they own, declares them, and runs the verifier and the audit on them as the web case did.

Presenter's notes. Offer to declare the first surface together.

The next step for a programmer is to put the verifier in CI.

A programmer checks a declaration of their own and then gates a build on the verifier's exit code.

Presenter's notes. The Build page lists the commands.

The next step for an AI builder is to hold a generator to the grammar and the verifier.

An AI builder gives a generator the grammar, then gives it the verifier's findings by rule number, and sees what the generator fixes.

Presenter's notes. The Generate page describes the loop.

The next step for a decision maker is to read the status board.

The status board shows what exists, what is designed and what is held, part by part, and where each part stops.

Presenter's notes. Point at the held rows, which show that nothing is hidden.
