Law · for people who review an interface and cite what it breaks

A rule of the Zane law is one entry of a versioned file that says what to avoid, what to do instead, why, and what earned it a number.

A rule is one entry of the file that holds the Zane law, and it states a defect to avoid and its remedy so that a reviewer or a tool can decide a case yes or no and cite it. The rules let a reviewer name exactly what an interface breaks, by number and title, instead of arguing about taste. The law holds 123 rules in 31 sets under 7 articles, at v3.16. This page shows what one rule holds, how many rules a machine can refuse today, and how the law changes, which happens only from recorded evidence.

The fields of one rule, as the tools load it

Rule 79, The Undo Covenant, is the rule the verifier cites when an act has no way back, as the Language page shows. The excerpt below shows fields of its entry in the file, with the long solution text left out:

"number": "79",
"title": "The Undo Covenant",
"avoid": [
  "Confirmation dialogs for every deletion",
  "No way to reverse common destructive actions",
…
"why": "Undo trusts first and interrupts never; confirmation interrupts always and trusts nothing.",
"derived_from": "reversibility",
"enforcement": "machine",
"evidence": "v1.3 practice addition; narrative constitution Art. IV.",
  • avoid and a solution make the rule something that a reviewer can decide yes or no.
  • why and derived_from tie the rule to one of the 7 articles, here Reversibility, so that a rule is a case of a principle and never a matter of taste.
  • enforcement says who can catch a violation today. Here the value is machine, because the verifier refuses a declaration that has an act and no undo.
  • evidence says what earned the rule a number. The evidence for this rule is a general practice addition and not a defect recorded in a real build, which is a thinner origin, and the file states it as it is.

Because every consumer loads the same file, the Reader that renders the rule, the verifier that refuses for it and the finding that cites it cannot drift apart.

Who can catch a violation of each rule today

Each rule declares one rung of an enforcement ladder, and rules climb as instruments are invented. The census is read from the file when the page loads.

RungRulesShareWho catches it
machine119%A conforming compiler or linter refuses it, today.
tool76%A runnable instrument measures it, such as the UI audit.
machine-later2520%It is decidable, and awaits its instrument.
judgment8065%A reviewer decides yes or no and cites the number.

Most of the law therefore waits on a person. The project is built to move this figure, and the page does not hide it. A rule that climbs a rung shows the argument working, and a rule that stays at machine-later shows that an instrument is missing. These figures were typed by hand until August 2026 and had gone stale, and they are now computed.

The operator rights and forbidden patterns that the rules serve

Across the rules, the law guarantees 6 operator rights, each traceable to the rules that serve it, and it names 17 forbidden patterns outright, for instance a selection that silently executes and a flow with no exit. A refusal cites these rights and patterns, and the right to leave in the verifier's output is one of the six rights. The Reader lists all of them.

How the rules of the law change

The seventh article of the law is Amendment. In its own words, the law grows only from evidence of real use, operator feedback and documented failure, and a trend or a developer's convenience is not a ground. Removing a protection needs proof that the harm is gone. In practice, a change to the rules follows five steps:

  1. A defect is recorded when it happens. The newest rules were canonized from defects found while building real applications, and the evidence table names the latest eight.
  2. The defect is kept only if it generalises and can be decided yes or no. A rule that a reviewer could only argue about does not get a number.
  3. The rule is numbered and placed in a set. Retired numbers (5 so far) and burned ones (2) are never reused, so a citation means the same thing for ever.
  4. The version of the law moves, and the pack's hash moves with it. A consumer that pins the pack sees the change as a different hash, and even one trailing space in the file gives a different hash.
  5. The rule's tier is stated, and the tier may climb. A rule declares who can catch it today and moves up when an instrument is invented: Rule 107 began as judgment and is now machine-checked, and Rule 62 became a tool rule once a runnable contrast check existed.

The record of passes is in the amendment docket and the lineage line of rules.json. The most recent change, to v3.16, allowed this site's navigation to have two horizontal levels.

This section describes how the project's own law has changed, and the project ruled every change by the process described here, which is written down for any team to use.
