Law · for people who review an interface and cite what it breaks

A rule says what to avoid, what to do instead, why, and what earned it a number.

The law is one versioned file: 123 rules in 31 sets under 7 articles, v3.13. This page shows what one rule holds, how many of them a machine can refuse today, and how the law changes, which is only from recorded evidence.

One rule, as the tools load it

Rule 79 is the one the verifier cited in What it refuses. These are fields of its entry in the file, with the long solution text left out:

"number": "79",
"title": "The Undo Covenant",
"avoid": [
  "Confirmation dialogs for every deletion",
  "No way to reverse common destructive actions",
…
"why": "Undo trusts first and interrupts never; confirmation interrupts always and trusts nothing.",
"derived_from": "reversibility",
"enforcement": "machine",
"evidence": "v1.3 practice addition; narrative constitution Art. IV.",
  • avoid and a solution make it something a reviewer can decide yes or no.
  • why and derived_from tie it to one of the 7 articles, here Reversibility, so a rule is a case of a principle and never a taste.
  • enforcement says who can catch a violation today. Here it is machine: the verifier refuses a declaration with an act and no undo.
  • evidence says what earned it a number. This one's is a general practice addition, not a defect recorded in a real build, which is a thinner pedigree; the file states it as it is.

Because every consumer loads the same file, the Reader that renders the rule, the verifier that refuses for it and the finding that cites it cannot drift apart.

Who can catch a violation, today

Each rule declares one rung of an enforcement ladder, and rules climb as instruments are invented. The census is read from the file when the page loads.

RungRulesShareWho catches it
machine119%A conforming compiler or linter refuses it, today.
tool76%A runnable instrument measures it, such as the UI audit.
machine-later2520%It is decidable, and awaits its instrument.
judgment8065%A reviewer decides yes or no and cites the number.

So most of the law waits on a person. That is the figure the project is built to move, and the page does not hide it: a rule that climbs a rung is the argument working, and one stuck at machine-later is the law admitting an instrument is missing. These figures were typed by hand until August 2026 and had gone stale; they are computed now.

Rights and forbidden patterns

Across the rules the law guarantees 6 operator rights, each traceable to the rules that serve it, and it names 17 forbidden patterns outright, for instance selection that silently executes and a flow with no exit. A refusal cites them: the right to leave in the verifier's output is one of the six. The Reader lists all of them.

How it changes

The seventh article is Amendment. In its own words the law grows only from evidence of real use, operator feedback and documented failure, and a trend or a developer's convenience is not a ground. Removing a protection needs proof that the harm is gone. In practice a change goes like this:

  1. A defect is recorded when it happens. The newest rules were canonized from defects found while building real applications, and the evidence table names the latest eight.
  2. It is kept only if it generalises and can be decided yes or no. A rule that a reviewer could only argue about does not get a number.
  3. It is numbered and placed in a set. Retired numbers (5 so far) and burned ones (2) are never reused, so a citation means the same thing for ever.
  4. The version moves and the pack's hash with it. A consumer that pins the pack sees the change as a different hash, and one trailing space in the file is a different hash too.
  5. Its tier is stated, and may climb. A rule declares who can catch it today and moves up when an instrument is invented: Rule 107 began as judgment and is now machine-checked, and Rule 62 became a tool rule once a runnable contrast check existed.

The record of passes is in the amendment docket and the lineage line of rules.json. The most recent change, to v3.13, is what allowed this site's navigation to have two horizontal levels.

Limit: this describes how the project's own law has changed, and it was ruled by the project. It is not a process that other teams have used, and nothing here shows that anyone outside it has proposed an amendment.
